## Efficiency of irrigation methods

Application efficiency is the share of applied water that ends up in the root zone. Typical ranges
are about 50 to 70 % for surface (furrow and flood) irrigation, 70 to 85 % for sprinklers and centre
pivots, and 85 to 95 % for well-maintained drip. The losses are evaporation, wind drift, runoff and
deep drainage below the roots. A poorly maintained system can fall well below these ranges.

## Time of day

Sprinkler and pivot irrigation loses less water early in the morning, when wind and evaporation are
lowest; irrigating in strong afternoon wind and heat wastes water to drift and evaporation and
lowers uniformity. As a rough guide, uniformity suffers noticeably when winds exceed about 15 to
20 km/h. Drip irrigation is less affected by timing, which can instead follow power availability
(solar hours or off-peak tariffs).

## Matching the application rate to the soil

Water applied faster than the soil can absorb runs off or ponds. Clay soils absorb slowly, sandy
soils quickly. On slow soils, split long irrigations into shorter cycles with a pause between them.
On sandy soils with little storage, irrigate more often with smaller depths so water is not lost
below the roots.

## Deficit irrigation

When water is scarce, applying less than full crop demand during the less sensitive growth stages,
and protecting the sensitive stages (usually flowering and early yield formation), keeps most of the
yield with less water. It needs good soil-moisture monitoring, because the margin for error is
smaller.

## Measuring what was applied

Recording each irrigation (start, duration and ideally metered volume) keeps the water balance
accurate, shows where water goes, and is needed to check use against licensed allocations.
