## Energy per cubic metre is the earliest warning

As a centrifugal pump wears (impeller erosion, growing wear-ring clearances), more water recirculates
inside it and it delivers less flow and head for the same power. Energy per cubic metre pumped
(kWh/m³) therefore creeps up long before the pump fails. A sustained rise of 10 % above the pump's
healthy baseline is worth investigating, and 20 % or more usually justifies inspecting the impeller
and wear rings. Pumping costs can rise noticeably before anything sounds wrong.

## Vibration

Vibration velocity (mm/s RMS) measured on the bearing housings is the standard condition indicator.
ISO 10816-3 grades it in zones; for medium-sized machines on flexible mounts the boundaries are
roughly 2.3 mm/s (newly commissioned), 4.5 mm/s (acceptable for long-term operation) and 7.1 mm/s
(above which damage is likely). A rising trend matters as much as the level. Common causes are
misalignment, worn bearings, imbalance from a damaged or clogged impeller, loose mounts and
cavitation.

## Cavitation

Cavitation happens when pressure at the pump inlet falls low enough for vapour bubbles to form and
collapse. It sounds like gravel passing through the pump, causes vibration, reduces flow and pits
the impeller. Typical causes are a blocked suction strainer or foot valve, too high a suction lift
(for example a falling water level in a borehole or dam), air leaks on the suction side and running
far from the pump's design point.

## Bearings, seals and motor temperature

Worn bearings show as noise, heat and higher vibration; follow the manufacturer's lubrication
interval and quantity, as both too little and too much grease cause failures. A mechanical seal
that drips more than the manufacturer allows should be replaced before it fails fully. A motor that
runs hotter than usual under the same load may have blocked cooling, a voltage problem, an
overload from a worn pump, or failing bearings.

## What an inspection covers

Listen and feel for unusual noise and vibration; check for leaks at the seal and pipework; confirm
suction and delivery pressures against normal values; measure flow and power to compute energy per
cubic metre; check the motor current and temperature; clean strainers and filters; and check that
mounts and couplings are tight and aligned. Recording each check builds the trend that predictive
maintenance relies on.

## Running hours, starts and service intervals

Wear accumulates with running hours, and frequent starts stress motors and couplings. Follow the
manufacturer's service interval in days or run-hours, whichever comes first, and bring it forward
for pumps that run long hours, handle sandy water or show a worsening trend.
